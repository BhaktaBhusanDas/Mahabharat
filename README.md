# The Mahabharata: A Kingdom Won, a World Lost

*An approximately 10-minute retelling.*

The Mahabharata is the story of a family that destroys itself over a kingdom. It is also a story about duty, love, pride, and the difficulty of doing what is right when every choice carries a cost.

Traditionally attributed to the sage Vyasa, the epic contains far more than its central war: philosophical dialogues, legends, pilgrimages, and stories within stories. This condensed retelling follows its main narrative. Details vary across traditions and translations.

## The princes of Hastinapura

The trouble began long before the battlefield.

Bhishma, a prince of the Kuru dynasty, renounced both the throne and marriage so that his father could marry the woman he loved. His terrible vow secured the succession for another branch of the family, but bound him to protect a royal house whose choices he would not always approve.

In time, two brothers stood at the centre of that house: Dhritarashtra, who was blind, and Pandu, who became king. When Pandu withdrew to the forest under a curse that made marital intimacy fatal, Dhritarashtra governed Hastinapura.

Through divine intervention, Pandu's wives, Kunti and Madri, bore five sons: Yudhishthira, Bhima, Arjuna, Nakula, and Sahadeva. They became known as the Pandavas. After Pandu's death, Kunti brought the boys back to the royal household.

There they grew up alongside Dhritarashtra's sons, the Kauravas, led by Duryodhana.

The cousins studied under the same teachers, including the formidable Drona. Arjuna became an extraordinary archer; Bhima possessed immense strength; Yudhishthira earned a reputation for truthfulness. Duryodhana saw their gifts and popularity as threats to his own claim to power.

His resentment hardened into hatred.

At a public display of martial skill, a warrior named Karna challenged Arjuna. When Karna's lineage was questioned, Duryodhana immediately made him a king. That act won Karna's enduring loyalty.

Almost nobody knew that Karna was Kunti's firstborn son, born before her marriage through an invocation to the sun god. Fearing disgrace, she had abandoned him as an infant. A charioteer and his wife had raised him. The rival Arjuna now faced was his own elder brother.

## Fire, marriage, and a new kingdom

Duryodhana's faction arranged for the Pandavas to stay in a palace built from highly flammable materials. The intention was to burn them alive.

Warned by their uncle Vidura, they escaped through a tunnel and disappeared into hiding with Kunti. For a time, the world believed them dead.

While travelling in disguise, the brothers attended the marriage contest of Draupadi, daughter of King Drupada. Arjuna completed the difficult archery challenge and won her hand.

When the brothers returned, they called to Kunti to see what they had brought. Before looking, she told them to share it equally. What followed was an extraordinary marriage: Draupadi became the wife of all five brothers, an arrangement the narrative explains through family deliberation and sacred precedents.

She would become one of the epic's fiercest voices, unwilling to let powerful men disguise injustice as duty.

With the Pandavas alive and supported by new allies, an uneasy settlement became necessary. The kingdom was divided. They received Khandavaprastha and developed it into the splendid capital of Indraprastha.

Their cousin Krishna became their trusted friend and guide. To the epic's devotional vision, he was also the divine present within human affairs.

Yudhishthira's influence grew. He performed the great royal sacrifice called the Rajasuya, asserting his sovereign standing. Duryodhana visited his cousins' magnificent court and saw everything he feared: their wealth, their alliances, their success.

He returned home determined to take it from them.

## The dice game

Duryodhana's uncle Shakuni offered a weapon more effective than an army: dice.

Yudhishthira accepted an invitation to play at Hastinapura. Bound by ideas of royal conduct, but also vulnerable to gambling, he entered a contest in which Shakuni played for Duryodhana and controlled the game.

Yudhishthira lost his riches, his kingdom, his brothers, and himself. Then he staked Draupadi.

Summoned to the assembly as though she were property, she demanded an answer: if Yudhishthira had already lost himself, what right remained to him to wager her?

The elders struggled or failed to answer. Their learning offered her no protection.

Dushasana, Duryodhana's brother, dragged her into the hall. Karna joined in her humiliation. In the widely told account, when Dushasana attempted to strip her, divine protection made her garment endless.

Bhima swore terrible revenge. Draupadi's question remained an accusation against the entire court.

Alarmed, Dhritarashtra restored the Pandavas' freedom and possessions. Yet another game followed, with another disastrous stake: twelve years of exile, followed by a thirteenth year living unrecognized. Discovery during that final year would mean renewed exile.

The Pandavas lost again.

They left for the forest, and the royal household's failure went with them.

## Thirteen years away

Exile tested the brothers in different ways. Bhima wanted immediate revenge. Draupadi challenged Yudhishthira's patience. Yudhishthira insisted that they honour the agreement, even though deceit had brought it about.

They encountered sages, heard ancient stories, and prepared for the struggle ahead. Arjuna obtained celestial weapons. The years became an education in power and restraint, suffering and responsibility.

One day, four brothers collapsed beside a lake after ignoring an unseen being's demand that they answer his questions before drinking. Yudhishthira stopped and listened.

Asked about the greatest wonder, he observed that people see others die every day, yet live as though they themselves will escape death.

Given the choice of restoring one brother, he chose Nakula. Kunti already had a living son in Yudhishthira; justice, he reasoned, required that Madri should have one too. Pleased, the questioner—revealed as Dharma, his divine father—restored all four.

In their thirteenth year, the Pandavas and Draupadi served in King Virata's household under disguises. They endured danger and indignity without revealing themselves until the required period had passed.

Then they asked for their kingdom back.

Duryodhana refused.

## The last chance for peace

Krishna went to the Kaurava court to seek a settlement. The Pandavas were prepared to accept even five villages. Duryodhana would not yield so much as a needle-point of land.

War became inevitable.

Both sides sought Krishna's aid. He offered a choice: his army, or himself, unarmed and taking no part in combat. Arjuna chose Krishna. Duryodhana received the army.

Krishna became Arjuna's charioteer.

Before the fighting, Kunti approached Karna and revealed that he was her son. The truth gave him a family at the moment when accepting it would mean abandoning the friend who had stood beside him.

He refused to betray Duryodhana. But he promised to spare four Pandavas. Only Arjuna would remain his chosen rival: one of the two would die, and Kunti would still have five sons.

On the plain of Kurukshetra, armies gathered from across the land. Teachers faced pupils. Uncles faced nephews. Friends stood beneath opposing banners.

Seeing them, Arjuna lowered his bow.

## Arjuna's doubt

What good was a kingdom purchased by killing those he loved? Arjuna asked Krishna. He would rather abandon the battle than win such a victory.

Krishna's response forms the Bhagavad Gita.

He spoke of the enduring self, of action and its consequences, of devotion, knowledge, and disciplined duty. Arjuna could not escape moral responsibility simply by refusing to act. He must judge and perform his duty without making personal reward the purpose of his actions.

Krishna also revealed his universal form: creation and destruction held within a reality far greater than the warrior before him could comprehend.

The dialogue did not make the coming deaths painless. It gave Arjuna a way to act within a world where action could not be made free of loss.

He lifted his bow.

## Eighteen days

The war lasted eighteen days. Its rules deteriorated as grief and desperation deepened.

Bhishma commanded the Kaurava army first. The Pandavas could scarcely overcome him. At last, Arjuna advanced with Shikhandi before him, knowing Bhishma would not fight Shikhandi because of a history reaching into a previous life.

Arjuna's arrows brought Bhishma down. He remained alive on a bed of arrows, able to choose the moment of his death.

Drona took command. During his leadership, Arjuna's young son Abhimanyu entered a complex battle formation that he knew how to penetrate but not how to escape. The warriors who should have followed him were blocked.

Surrounded, he fought until he was overwhelmed and killed. Several great warriors participated in the assault. His death made the war still more bitter.

To defeat Drona, the Pandavas attacked the attachment that sustained him: his love for his son Ashwatthama. Bhima killed an elephant with that name. Yudhishthira then declared that Ashwatthama was dead, adding words identifying the elephant that Drona did not clearly hear.

Trusting Yudhishthira's reputation for truth, Drona laid down his weapons. Draupadi's brother Dhrishtadyumna killed him.

Even the most truthful Pandava had now helped turn deception into a weapon.

Karna eventually took command. Earlier, he had used his irreplaceable divine weapon to kill Ghatotkacha, Bhima's powerful son, saving the Kaurava army but surrendering the weapon he had reserved for Arjuna.

When the rivals finally met, Karna's chariot wheel became trapped in the earth. He asked for a pause while he freed it. Krishna reminded him of Draupadi's humiliation and Abhimanyu's killing: where had this regard for righteousness been then?

At Krishna's urging, Arjuna struck and killed him.

Bhima killed Dushasana, fulfilling his vow of vengeance. At last, Duryodhana stood almost alone. In a mace duel, Bhima struck his thigh, violating the rules of that combat and fulfilling another oath made after Draupadi's humiliation.

The Pandavas had won. The means of victory would follow them home.

## The night after victory

The killing did not end with the battle.

Ashwatthama, burning to avenge his father, attacked the sleeping Pandava camp at night with two surviving allies. Among the dead were Dhrishtadyumna and Draupadi's five sons.

The Pandavas survived because they were elsewhere. Their children did not.

Later, Ashwatthama directed a devastating weapon against the unborn child of Abhimanyu's widow, Uttara. Krishna preserved the child, Parikshit, who would continue the dynasty.

Victory had narrowed to a fragile future in a mother's womb.

Gandhari, who had lost her sons, confronted Krishna. In her grief, she cursed him: his own people would one day destroy one another, just as the Kurus had done.

Kunti revealed Karna's identity to the Pandavas. Their triumph acquired a new horror. They had killed their elder brother without knowing him.

Yudhishthira no longer wanted the throne. What could kingship mean amid so many funerals?

Persuaded that the survivors still needed a ruler, he accepted it. Before Bhishma finally died, Yudhishthira learned from him about government, duty, and the responsibilities of power.

Ruling would be another burden to carry.

## The final journey

Years passed. Gandhari's curse came to fruition: Krishna's people destroyed one another in an internal conflict. Krishna himself departed the world after a hunter's arrow struck him.

The Pandavas understood that their own time was ending.

They placed Parikshit on the throne and set out with Draupadi toward the Himalayas, leaving royal life behind. A dog followed them.

One by one, Draupadi and four brothers fell. Yudhishthira continued, accompanied only by the animal.

When Indra offered to take him to heaven, Yudhishthira refused to abandon the dog. He would not desert a creature that had trusted him merely to secure his own happiness.

The dog revealed itself as Dharma. The journey had held one final test.

Even heaven brought questions. Yudhishthira saw Duryodhana honoured and was then shown his own loved ones suffering. He chose to remain with them rather than enjoy paradise alone. This, too, proved a test; the appearances passed, and the earthly antagonists were ultimately reconciled beyond their mortal lives.

The Mahabharata ends beyond the contest for the throne. Its heroes have shown courage and generosity, but also weakness and cruelty. Its elders have known what was right and still failed to defend it. Its victories have demanded more than anyone expected to pay.

After armies, vows, and kingdoms, one of its last acts of righteousness is quiet and small: a tired man refusing to abandon a dog.
